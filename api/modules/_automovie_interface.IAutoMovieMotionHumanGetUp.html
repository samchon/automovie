    Namespace IAutoMovieMotionHumanGetUp

    Getting up from the floor to standing: from lying on the back or the front, from the hands and knees or crouched, as a crawl ends.

    Rising from the floor is described by the movement of three body regions (VanSant 1988, Phys Ther 68:185, "Rising from a supine position to erect stance"): the arms, the head and trunk (the axial region) and the legs. The one combination VanSant found most often among adults, though in only a quarter of them, is symmetrical throughout: the trunk flexes forward out of supine with both hands pushing, the body comes through sitting to a squat and stands. That is the symmetric pattern, the default from the back. The other end of VanSant's axial scale is a full rotation: the body rolls over to face the floor, comes up onto the hands and knees, through a half kneel to standing; that is the rotation pattern, the only way up from the front and from the hands and knees, whose first postures it has already passed.

    The rise is a support sequence (support): each posture of the route is a stance resting on what that posture bears on (BEARS), its key solved by the whole-body fit nearest the route's posture (POSES, provisional angles, with lieDown's own floor postures where they are the same) with those parts on the floor and the centre of mass over them, a new support placed before an old one lets go, the body carried between keys about a support both hold and resting on the floor by its colliders at every instant. The first stance leaves from the posture the entry displays, so the rise continues from where the body lies.

    Young adults rise from supine to standing in 2.6 s on average at their own pace (Ulbrich et al. 2000, J Am Geriatr Soc 48:1626); the route's phases share that time equally (provisional split), scaled by the manner's tempo. Events: one per posture reached, then standing.

    Not yet: a partial rotation (one hand pushing from the side) and the half kneel out of the symmetric squat, VanSant's other adult variants.
