    Namespace IAutoMovieMotionHumanWalkPace

    The timing of a stepping gait over a distance: its step period, its swing, and how far along its path the pelvis is at each instant.

    The step period is the pace's own, the time its longest step takes at the asked speed, whatever the distance: a distance that is not a whole number of longest steps is walked in shorter steps at the same cadence, and so a little slower, as a walker regulates the last steps to a target by their length (Lee, Lishman and Thomson 1982, J Exp Psychol Hum Percept Perform 8:448). A period shrunk with the step instead would quicken the cadence by a fifth on a 3 m walk of five steps where four fall just short, and without bound on a short one.

    The pelvis gathers its speed over a ramp, cruises one step per period and sheds the speed over a second ramp, each ramp a smooth step of the speed (zero rate and zero rate of rate at both ends). At a heel strike the landing footprint lies front steps ahead of the pelvis. A gait with a flight passes at a foot's mid-stance over the middle of the way its centre of pressure travels, from the heel to the ball: the spring-mass leg of running is symmetric about its contact (Blickhan 1989, J Biomech 22:1217), so the leg lands as far ahead of that contact as it leaves it behind. That puts the landing 1 - swing / 2T steps ahead less the lead the middle lies ahead of the ankle; a gait with double support shares the step between the two legs, so the footprint lies no more than half a step ahead. Cruising, the pelvis is j - front steps along at heel strike j when the ramp up lasts twice the time from the start to 1 - front periods before the first heel strike, and it ends over the closing pair when the ramp down lasts 2 (1 - front) periods.

    The ramp up lasts longer wherever that would carry the pelvis past the foot left standing at the start, by the time it lifts one period after the first toe-off, further than a cruising stance carries it past its footprint: a leg trails no further at the start than in full stride. The period then lengthens so the longer ramp does not quicken the cruise, and a plan too short for both ramps shrinks them in proportion.

    A walk whose way is set from outside (IProps.travel, a cadenced station) keeps the asked period and as many steps as the way takes to be gone, and its pelvis is where the way says: each step is then as long as the way gone in it, as a drilled soldier keeps his place at a called cadence by the length of his step.
