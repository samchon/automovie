    Namespace IAutoMovieMotionHumanStepOver

    Stepping over a low obstacle placed in the scene, a kerb or a hurdle (HumanObstacle), from standing in front of it: the lead foot lifts over it and lands beyond, then the trail foot follows and lands beside it, both feet clearing the obstacle's top.

    The goals are footprints and clearance: each swinging foot takes the shortest path from its footprint to its landing that keeps every sole point over the obstacle at least MARGIN above its top and every other one on or above the floor, the taut string over the obstacle (AutoMovieMotionHumanClearance), travelled on minimum-jerk progress. Patla and Rietdyk (1993, Gait Posture 1:45) found that the lead toe clears an obstacle by a roughly constant margin whatever its height, so the lift grows with the obstacle and the margin does not; 0.1 m is the margin used (provisional, of the order they report). The lead foot lifts its toes by up to TOE_UP as they pass over the near side and is level again once its heel does, so the toe clears the edge with the ankle and the knee lower than a level foot would need; where the ankle cannot take the planned pitch and the toes droop, the ankle is raised until every sole point clears. The lead foot lands so the obstacle's middle falls at mid-swing, at least one tenth of a foot length beyond the far side and no farther than the legs' stride allows; the trail foot lands beside it at the body's rest separation.

    While the lead foot swings, the trail foot rises on its ball by PUSH, the forefoot rocker of a stance foot the body passes over (Perry and Burnfield), and the pelvis rises with it as far as it lifts that ankle, so the lead hip stands higher over the obstacle; the trail foot later leaves from that pitch and levels by the time its toe reaches the near side. The pelvis stays back over the trail foot until the lead foot is crossing, moves halfway between the feet by the double support and over the landed feet while the trail foot swings, each leg on a minimum-jerk path. It is lowered wherever a planted leg could not reach its foot, and over the second half of each swing toward where the swinging leg will reach its landing, so it has come down when the foot lands instead of dropping at the contact.

    Time scales with leg length by dynamic similarity (Alexander 1984): the whole action takes 2.4 sqrt(l / 0.93) seconds for a hip height l (provisional: a deliberate two-step crossing near 1 s a step for an adult) times the manner's tempo, the lead swinging over 8 to 42 % and the trail over 50 to 82 %, the pelvis staying between the feet until 70 % so the trail limb crosses with the hip above the obstacle, where hip and knee flexion can lift its toe (trail-limb clearance comes from hip and knee flexion, Chou and Draganich 1997, J Biomech 30:331), then moving over the landed feet.

    The obstacle lies ahead along the performer's heading; its near side must be within one leg length of the feet and its height at most 0.55 of the hip height (provisional, near the hip flexion a standing step can use), otherwise the step is refused with the limits. Events: leadToeOff, leadContact, trailToeOff, trailContact.
