    Namespace support

    Moving the body's weight across a sequence of supports: each stance's contacts (HumanSupport.IStance) solved into one static key posture, and the body carried from key to key with every new contact placed and bearing before any contact the next stance lacks lets go.

    A key posture is the static multi-contact posture nearest the posture the body enters with that puts every contact on its target and keeps every joint in its range (HumanBodyFit, Bouyarmane and Kheddar 2010, Escande et al. 2013). An explicit positive balance request uses the fitted world contacts and the selected part on the prepared clock. Each new key is solved from the key before it, as a planner grows its next stance from its parent. Between two stances the planner solves a third key on the union of both stances' contacts, so the new contacts bear before the old ones release (Escande et al.'s transition configurations, in both stances at once), and the body moves between keys on a minimum-jerk course in joint space (Flash and Hogan 1985), each joint on its own range (blendHumanJoint). A part that bears in two stances in a row on different targets steps: the planner first solves a key without its old contact, the part over the middle of its step lifted a stepping limb's rise (crawl.LIFT) clear of the higher end and bearing on nothing, then the transition key with it set down, so a contact is broken before it is made again. Along the joint-space course the root keeps the first common contact on its point, and every held hand and foot is then fitted by its own two-bone chain to where the two keys hold it (prepareSupportInterval); a held knee or trunk contact rides the root pin and the floor bound alone. A contact no key posture reaches is refused with the contact, its stance and the miss.

    A bearing foot lies on the floor at its toes' joint with its toes flat; a bearing hand lies on its palm laid flat on the surface; on the floor a knee bears on its shin and the trunk on its colliders, resting on their lowest points, and on a raised surface each lays its own skin flat on it (the knee, the seat, the upper back or the abdomen); a contact held across stances without a place stays where it was first laid; a gripping hand closes on its target at its grip. Each move takes SECONDS (provisional: no study of the timing of a general support sequence was located) or its stance's own, a step's third of it each to lift, to set down and to settle. Events: lift<i> when the move into stance i has its stepping parts at the top of their swing, bear<i> when it has placed its new contacts, stance<i> (or the stance's own event) when stance i is held alone. The plan lets overlays move the head and any arm and hand that have no contact window in the sequence.
