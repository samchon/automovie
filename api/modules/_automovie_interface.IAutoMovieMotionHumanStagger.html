    Namespace IAutoMovieMotionHumanStagger

    A stagger: a blow pushes the centre of mass past the base of support and recovery steps catch it.

    The centre of mass moves over the floor as an inverted pendulum of Hof's leg length, 1.34 hip heights (Hof, Gazendam and Sinke 2005, J Biomech 38:1), from the blow's speed along the push, its centre of pressure always as near the extrapolated centre of mass x + v / omega0, omega0 = sqrt(g / l), as the bearing feet reach: on it, which holds it, or at the nearest point of their outline, from which it runs on (Hof 2008, Hum Mov Sci 27:112). Each recovery step chooses its time and its place within measured bounds, as capture-point stepping does (Koolen et al. 2012, Int J Robot Res 31:1094; Griffin et al. 2017): the weight shifts onto the stance foot over both feet, the foot swings in its direction's step time and lands the sole point it lands on as near the extrapolated centre of mass as its reachable footstep region allows, and the body is caught once the extrapolated centre of mass lies inside the base the two feet make. The region is the stance foot's: within its direction's longest recovery step of where the foot stood beside the stance foot; a step past it shortens toward the stance foot. The legs then admit each step at its mid-swing and its landing, under the pelvis the centre of mass carries there: each planted leg's reach, knee flexion and thigh extension as a two-bone chain, and crossed legs clear of each other as the tangle audit reads them (staggerAdmission, walkLegClearance). The legs may cross: a footstep planner's region on the stepping foot's own side of the stance foot (Deits and Tedrake 2014) keeps a walking robot's legs apart, but it forbids the crossover step people take in most lateral recoveries, so it is not this region. Every order of the feet over up to four steps is tried, the fewest steps first, so capture is monotone in the push's speed; the order preferred alternates its feet forward and backward (Maki and McIlroy 1997), beginning with the foot further along the push, the rear foot for a push backward, and pushed sideways begins with the leg the push unloads, which crosses in front of or behind the loaded one (Maki, McIlroy and Perry 1996: 96% of lateral stepping responses), the loaded leg's side-step being the other order. The bounds are those of younger adults at the threshold of balance recovery: an 82 ms reaction and a 138 ms weight shift before the first step (Cyr, Moglo and Smeesters 2005), steps of 238, 176 and 193 ms and up to 0.59, 0.38 and 0.42 statures forward, sideways and backward (Telonio, Cyr, Moglo and Smeesters 2005), read between those directions on an ellipse; the leg a sideways push unloads leaves the floor 20% sooner and swings 70% longer than a forward or backward step (Maki, McIlroy and Perry 1996, LATERAL). A foot stepping forward strikes with its heel and one stepping backward lands on its forefoot (Grasso, Bianchi and Lacquaniti 1998). A push four steps do not catch is a fall, as people take up to four recovery steps (Debelle, Maganaris and O'Brien 2021): it is refused, naming the fastest push the body catches that way.

    Once caught, the other foot closes at the entry's stance width, as far ahead or behind as stands the middle of the two ankles under the caught point as the entry stood over its own. The extrapolated centre of mass then tracks a minimum-jerk course to where the body stood over its feet, its centre of pressure placed by the divergent component's tracking law inside the base (Englsberger, Ott and Albu-Schaffer 2015), and the centre of mass follows it, x' = omega0 (xi - x), until both rest within a millimetre.

    The body over its feet is read at each second from prepared courses (StaggerFeet): the trunk bends about the hips against the fall, the hip strategy (Horak and Nashner 1986, J Neurophysiol 55:1369), by the inclination of the extrapolated centre of mass's lead over the pendulum's length, bounded by the hip's extension (staggerLean); the pelvis carries the centre of mass's course less the shift that tilt makes of it, and is lowered to the height its planted legs allow, as the walk settles its pelvis (walkReach.ground), each heel rising about its ball where the ankle runs out of room. A stance whose leg leans further to its side than the ankle's inversion or eversion rolls onto its edge (walkReachStance.SIDE); a stepping foot first rises clear of the floor by the minimum toe clearance of walking (walkFoot.CLEARANCE, Killeen et al. 2017), over the share of its swing a walking foot takes to set down from that height, then swings as a walking foot swings (walkFoot.prepareSwing) to the stance it lands in, held inside its leg's ranges and as high over the floor as its swing carries it (walkReach.swing), so it leaves and joins its stances where they stand.

    Both arms abduct as balance reactions, 9.4 degrees from 154 ms after the push (the mean of the two arms' abduction within 200 ms of a backward release, 10.7 and 8.0 degrees, at its measured onset; Weerdesteyn, Laing and Robinovitch 2008, Exp Brain Res 186:571), held through the steps and let down as the body settles, each let out further where it would pass into the body (HumanArmSwing.of). The steps, the catch and the settling are the body's dynamics, which no manner changes; the manner's tempo scales the righting.

    The push is read where its source is as the plan begins, the causal read a reaction makes (ReactionSource).
