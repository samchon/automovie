# Shots and cuts

How scripts and the screenplay decide what the camera shows and where the picture cuts, within a scene and between scenes. A shot is one unbroken view, and a cut ends it. [Staging](../../motion/staging.md#the-view) realizes these decisions and checks them on the captured frames.

## Choosing the shot

Choose each shot for what the audience must read at that moment. A wide or full shot shows where people are and how they move through the place, a medium shot an exchange between them, and a close shot the one reaction, object or contact the moment turns on. Reorient the audience with a wider view when a scene opens, when someone enters or when the layout changes, because a run of close shots loses the room.

Put the camera at eye height for an exchange between equals. Raise or lower it, or show one character's point of view, only where the balance between people or what one of them sees is the point of the moment.

A lens shapes perspective: a short focal length near the subject stretches depth and speed, and a long one far away flattens distance and isolates a face. Decide a lens only where that effect carries meaning, and leave it to the production otherwise.

Move the camera when something moves it, such as a subject it follows, a reveal or pressure that a slow push builds. A held frame lets the action inside it play, and the screenplay decides stillness as deliberately as movement.

## The line

Keep the camera on one side of the line through two people in an exchange, so each keeps the same side of the frame in every shot and their eyelines meet across the cut. Cross the line only on screen, by a camera move or a person crossing it, or as a disorientation the scene intends. Travel keeps its screen direction the same way: someone who leaves the frame to the right enters the next shot from the left.

An exchange is commonly cut between matching single shots or over-the-shoulder shots of each speaker, cutting to the listener when the reaction matters more than the line.

## Cutting

Cut when attention should move, to new information, a reaction or a better view of the action. Change the size or the angle by at least about 30 degrees around the subject at a cut, because a smaller change reads as a jump in the same shot.

Cut on an action, such as a turn, a sit or a strike, so the movement carries the eye across the join, with positions, gaze and held objects continuous on both sides. Let sound lead or trail a cut where it binds two shots, such as a line heard before its speaker appears.

Pace comes from shot length: shorter shots as pressure rises, longer ones for stillness, for tension the audience must feel and for an action that must stay whole. A cut is the default between scenes, and a dissolve, a fade or a match cut is a decision the scene earns. When a cut cannot keep everything, keep emotion, then story, then rhythm, before exact spatial continuity, as Walter Murch's *In the Blink of an Eye* ranks them.

## Frame

Compose for the delivered aspect ratio. A vertical frame fits one figure or a close pair best, so stage travel toward or away from the camera rather than across the frame.

## Writing them down

In the screenplay, write the view in each scene's standing state and its changes in the beat records, as [notation](notation.md#records) sets. Give each shot's size, subject and movement in the terms the installed camera grammar performs, as the interface package's types define them, and the camera's height, angle or side of the line in words where they matter, which [staging](../../motion/staging.md#the-view) realizes by placing the camera.
