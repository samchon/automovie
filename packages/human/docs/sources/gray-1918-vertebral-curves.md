# Gray's Anatomy (1918), the curves of the vertebral column

Gray H., Lewis W.H. (ed.) *Anatomy of the Human Body*, 20th ed., Lea & Febiger, 1918, section II.2b "The Vertebral Column as a Whole", paragraph "Curves". Read in the PubAnnotation digitisation of the text, https://pubannotation.org/docs/sourcedb/GrayAnatomy/sourceid/25 (the Bartleby rendering cited by `gray-1918.md` refused the request).

## Licence

Public domain (published 1918). Wording may be quoted freely.

## Protocol

Classical descriptive anatomy of the adult skeleton, viewed laterally. Grade: established anatomical account; no angles or depths are given.

## Content

- The column, viewed from the side, has four curves named for its regions: cervical, thoracic, lumbar and pelvic.
- Cervical: convex forward, from the apex of the odontoid process to the middle of the second thoracic vertebra; the least marked curve.
- Thoracic: concave forward, from the middle of the second to the middle of the twelfth thoracic vertebra; its most prominent point behind is the spinous process of the seventh thoracic vertebra.
- Lumbar: convex forward, from the middle of the last thoracic vertebra to the sacrovertebral angle; "more marked in the female than in the male"; the lower three vertebrae are much more convex than the upper two.
- Pelvic: from the sacrovertebral articulation to the point of the coccyx, its concavity directed downward and forward.
- The thoracic and pelvic curves are primary (present in fetal life); the cervical and lumbar are secondary, appearing when the child holds up its head and when it begins to walk.
- Average length of the column: about 71 cm in the male, 61 cm in the female.

## Use

Seen from behind on the skin's median line, the thoracic curve is convex backward with its apex near T7, the lumbar curve concave backward, and the sacrum convex backward again. The order of these apices from the buttocks to cervicale, and the sign of each curve against the chord of its neighbours, follow from the text; their size does not.

## Limits

No magnitudes: the text fixes the direction of each curve, not its angle or depth. Soft tissue over the spinous processes is thin but not uniform, so the skin's median line follows the curves without reproducing their radiographic angles.
