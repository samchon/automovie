## Different victories on land and sea {#egypt}

Target: `docs/body/screenplay/001-chronicle/003-army.md#egypt`. Inherited identities: `011-egypt-and-isolation.md#nile`; `003-army.md#egypt`.

EXT. EGYPT — DAY, 1798

French soldiers cross the dry ground. The men closest to us breathe through open mouths; beyond them, the army continues out of sight.

HISTORIAN (V.O.): France had sent them toward eastern commerce and Britain's position in India. Egypt was the undertaking's first object. Its possession would not fulfil the whole ambition.

The march gives way to battle at the Pyramids.

Shots break through smoke. Men close the spaces left by men who have fallen. From within the French ranks we see little beyond the next bodies and the ground underfoot; when the smoke thins, their opponents are giving up the field.

The firing becomes intermittent. French soldiers remain on the ground they have won. One man lowers his weapon with both hands, as though only now feeling its weight.

INT. AN OCCUPIED BUILDING — DAY

A resident stands beside belongings drawn close against a wall. A French soldier indicates what he wants taken. The resident pulls an item back against the pile. The soldier reaches past the resident's hand and takes it.

Across the room, a scholar waits for his assistant to reach the opposite wall. Their measuring line crosses the space the resident has just vacated.

SCHOLAR: Against the wall. All the way.

The assistant adjusts his end. The scholar reads the width and turns to the open record.

SCHOLAR: That is different.

ASSISTANT: Shall I put it down?

SCHOLAR: First do it again.

They reset the line. This time the scholar follows it with his eyes from one wall to the other before comparing the result with the earlier entry. He corrects the record and gestures toward the next part of the building. The assistant gathers the line.

The resident lifts the remaining belongings out of their way. The soldier carries the requisitioned property through the doorway. The survey continues behind him.

EXT. FRENCH ANCHORAGE, THE NILE — NIGHT

British ships move toward the French fleet. Orders pass across a British deck, partly lost under wind and the movement of rigging.

Nelson watches from the quarterdeck. His right sleeve has no arm inside it. With his left hand he directs attention toward the signal being prepared.

NELSON: Make it.

The signal rises. On another British ship, an officer looks up, recognizes it and turns to his own men.

OFFICER: Steady. Hold her steady.

The helmsman bears against the wheel. Sailors haul together; canvas changes its pull. A gun crew works in the confined space below, bringing a loaded gun out toward the dark shape of a French ship.

The gun fires. Recoil throws it back into the men waiting to receive it. Smoke rolls along the deck. Before it clears, they are preparing to fire again.

On the French side, answering guns flash through the darkness. British gunfire and sailors' work continue across successive ships, until the French fleet's defeat is visible in disabled vessels and fire upon the water.

Nelson receives a report amid the noise. He turns from the man toward the battle still being fought. The crews keep working.

INT. FRENCH HEADQUARTERS IN EGYPT — DAY, LATER

Napoleon listens to a man who has brought news from the coast.

REPORTING OFFICER: The British have defeated the fleet.

Napoleon waits. The man has finished.

NAPOLEON: Our ships?

REPORTING OFFICER: We cannot count on them to supply us. Or to take the army home.

Outside the room, soldiers are carrying supplies past the door. Napoleon watches them pass, then turns back to the officer.

NAPOLEON: What can still reach us?

REPORTING OFFICER: Less than before, General. I cannot promise how much.

Napoleon returns to the table and brings the papers concerning provisions in front of him. The reporting officer starts to withdraw.

NAPOLEON: Stay. We must go through this again.

Through the open doorway, soldiers keep carrying supplies while Napoleon takes up the first provision paper.

## People who keep revising a clause {#law}

Target: `docs/body/screenplay/001-chronicle/004-order.md#law`. Inherited identities: `017-law-and-crown.md#code`, `#emperor`; `004-order.md#law`.

INT. LEGAL DELIBERATION ROOM — FRANCE, 1804

The jurist speaking has a manuscript open before him. Other participants have reached the same passage. Napoleon presides at the table.

JURIST: Property needs protection whoever holds it. That has to remain clear when a judge opens this book without any of us beside him.

NAPOLEON: Then make it clear.

JURIST: We are trying to. But you keep answering the case in front of you. We are writing for cases we have not heard.

Napoleon leans forward to take up the argument. Cambacérès speaks before the two men can speak across each other.

CAMBACÉRÈS: Let him finish, First Consul. Then we can decide what needs changing.

SCREEN TEXT: Cambacérès — coordinator of the legal deliberations.

The jurist goes back through the passage, pointing out where its reach becomes uncertain. Napoleon follows on his own copy.

JURIST: If the general rule depends on the standing of the person who comes before the court, it will not be a general rule for long.

NAPOLEON: I want the rule to survive the people who make it.

CAMBACÉRÈS: Then we must settle the wording, not merely agree on the intention.

The jurist turns back a page. Another jurist points to an earlier correction.

SECOND JURIST: We have already changed this part. If we leave the later passage as it is, the two will pull against each other.

NAPOLEON: Which one would you change?

SECOND JURIST: The later one. Keep the protection we have agreed here.

Cambacérès makes room beside the manuscript. The jurist brings the two passages together where Napoleon can follow them.

The light at the windows changes. The discussion is still at the table.

INT. THE SAME ROOM — ANOTHER SITTING

There are corrections on the papers now being read. A jurist holds his place with one finger while Napoleon speaks.

NAPOLEON: Equality before the law does not abolish authority in the family.

JURIST: The wives and children governed by that authority will read the promise of equality too.

NAPOLEON: I am not proposing to free them from it.

The jurist looks down at the passage again. He begins to turn the page, then stops.

JURIST: Then this cannot be left for a reader to reconcile by goodwill.

CAMBACÉRÈS: Show us where you think the language blurs it.

The jurist indicates the passage. Cambacérès waits while the others find it.

CAMBACÉRÈS: Leave the authority in place. Revise this passage so that its reach is clear.

The jurist marks the work that remains. Another jurist asks to see it before they turn the page.

INT. THE SAME ROOM — LATER

A fresh disagreement has held the room. Napoleon is on his feet; the jurists remain seated around their papers.

JURIST: A worker comes under the general law. Yet we retain control over his labor.

NAPOLEON: It remains.

JURIST: Then we cannot write as though the general promise has removed it.

NAPOLEON: No. Show me where you think we have.

The man passes his copy to Napoleon and indicates the passage. Napoleon reads it, then sits.

NAPOLEON: This needs to be clearer.

CAMBACÉRÈS: We shall have the jurists redraft it and bring it back.

HISTORIAN (V.O.): Four jurists had drafted the code. Cambacérès was not one of them.

The manuscript being worked on becomes a completed printed volume. A reader opens it at a desk beyond the deliberation room.

HISTORIAN (V.O.): In 1804 it was completed and promulgated. The government had made a lasting body of law. What happened in each court would still depend on people applying it.

INT. GOVERNMENT ROOM — 1804

The discussion has moved from law to the survival of the regime. Napoleon listens while a participant addresses him across the table.

PARTICIPANT: The threats against you will not end. What happens to the government if they succeed?

HISTORIAN (V.O.): The regime used threats against it, and the Duke of Enghien's execution, to argue for hereditary rule. That argument defended the regime's security. It did not settle the justice of the execution.

PARTICIPANT: With a succession, the office would remain after the man.

NAPOLEON: And those who attack the man would know it.

Napoleon remains at the table as the discussion continues. Outside the room, an attendant admits the next person waiting to see the First Consul.

INT. FORMAL AUDIENCE — MAY 18, 1804

An official approaches Napoleon. The room waits for the form of address.

OFFICIAL: Your Imperial Majesty.

Napoleon receives him. The official bows before continuing. The next man to approach also bows.

SECOND OFFICIAL: Your Majesty.

INT. PALACE ROOM — LATER

Joséphine stands in the dress of her new public position. An attendant finishes arranging a jewel, then withdraws from between her and Napoleon.

JOSÉPHINE: Empress.

She says the title quietly, testing what now belongs to her.

NAPOLEON: Yes.

JOSÉPHINE: They will ask about a successor.

Napoleon remains beside her. Neither answers the question of an heir. The attendant returns to the ceremonial dress when Joséphine raises her arm to allow it.

INT. CORONATION CEREMONY — DECEMBER 2, 1804

Joséphine's jewels catch the light as she moves in her ceremonial dress. From the assembled witnesses, we see the public elevation of the Emperor and Empress.

The sound of the service ends before the next image.

EXT. DISTRIBUTION OF THE EAGLES — DECEMBER 5, 1804

Soldiers stand together as the eagles are distributed. The standards pass into military hands in a ceremony of their own.

INT. VIEW OF THE CORONATION PAINTING — LATER

The painted ceremony fills the image. Among its spectators is Napoleon's mother, Letizia.

HISTORIAN (V.O.): His mother had not attended. The later painting gave her a place there.

We draw close enough to see the paint in the face that the actual ceremony did not contain.

## An army remains {#eylau-and-tilsit}

Target: `docs/body/screenplay/001-chronicle/005-map.md#eylau-and-tilsit`. Inherited identities: `022-blood-and-tilsit.md#eylau`, `#tilsit`, `#new-map`; `005-map.md#eylau-and-tilsit`.

EXT. EYLAU — SNOWSTORM, 1807

Infantry emerge from the snow and vanish into it again. A man looks toward the men beside him for direction; their line is already passing out of his sight to the left.

HISTORIAN (V.O.): Augereau's corps lost its direction in the storm and drifted left.

Russian artillery fires into the infantry. The advancing bodies break apart under the fire. Men who have turned to find their place meet others trying to get away. The attack collapses before the camera leaves them.

Beyond the fugitives, cavalry gathers motion. Murat is among the horsemen as they go forward.

We remain low enough to feel horses passing close, then follow their movement into the Russian lines. The riders push through. From farther back, the passage opens and disappears amid men, horses and smoke.

The sound of hooves recedes. Gunfire continues.

EXT. EYLAU — AFTER THE FIGHTING

A wounded man tries to draw his clothing over himself. His fingers fail to hold the cloth. Another wounded body lies beyond his reach.

The wind moves loose fabric against the snow. French soldiers still hold their ground.

Across the field, Russian soldiers are together under their officers. Men move among them carrying the business of an army still in being.

HISTORIAN (V.O.): The French had held. The cavalry's intervention had not destroyed Russia's forces. For the wounded left in winter, surviving the fighting was only the first danger.

EXT. FRENCH ARMY — LATER IN 1807

SCREEN TEXT: Friedland — French victory.

The army moves on from another battle. News of victory is being carried away from it.

HISTORIAN (V.O.): Friedland brought a French victory before the emperors met at Tilsit. It brought Napoleon to the meeting with power. Alexander still had to choose what Russia would do.

INT. NEGOTIATION AT TILSIT — 1807

Napoleon and Alexander face one another across a table. Napoleon sits forward; Alexander rests against the back of his chair.

NAPOLEON: We can end the war here.

ALEXANDER: You have won a battle. Russia will still have interests when we leave this room.

NAPOLEON: I know that.

ALEXANDER: I will not make peace by placing Russia at your disposal.

NAPOLEON: I am asking you to settle with me.

Alexander waits for him to go on. Napoleon lets the answer stand.

ALEXANDER: And afterwards?

NAPOLEON: You will still answer for Russia. I shall have to deal with your government.

ALEXANDER: I can refuse this peace.

NAPOLEON: Yes. Will you agree?

Napoleon waits. Alexander considers the man opposite him, then brings his chair closer to the table.

ALEXANDER: Yes. Russia is better served by an agreement than by continuing this war.

NAPOLEON: Then we are agreed.

Alexander offers his hand. Napoleon takes it. We stay with both men as the handshake ends and they remain together to conclude the settlement.

INT. TILSIT — LATER

Louise, Queen of Prussia, addresses Napoleon.

LOUISE: Prussia must survive this. Its dynasty must survive.

NAPOLEON: I have heard your request, Madame.

Louise stays where she is. Napoleon's acknowledgment has given her no answer about what she seeks. We remain with her until the movement of people beyond her draws his attention away.

HISTORIAN (V.O.): Prussia could not bargain as an equal. Its humiliation did not end the country or the work its people would do.

EXT. TERRITORIES CHANGED BY FRENCH POWER — SUCCESSIVE DAYS

People wait where the business of government is conducted. One is admitted to work that gives him a place he did not have before. Elsewhere, a declaration of changed personal status is read to people who listen, then return to obligations still waiting for them.

HISTORIAN (V.O.): Some gained status, release from personal subordination, or a path upward. Freedom in one relation did not extinguish every obligation.

Money is collected. Men leave those who have come to see them off. The administration that offers advancement also requires payment and bodies for France's wars.

INT. PRUSSIAN WORKING ROOMS — OVER TIME

Officers and civil officials work among papers, interrupt one another, return to unfinished business. A discussion in one room carries into another sitting with other participants.

HISTORIAN (V.O.): Prussian reorganization took collective work over time. The limit on its retained army strength came in 1808, after Tilsit.

The officials continue. Outside, men are leaving for service while money is collected from those who remain.

## Changing the king does not end a country {#spain}

Target: `docs/body/screenplay/001-chronicle/005-map.md#spain`. Inherited identities: `024-spain-will-not-close.md#bayonne`, `#bailen`, `#peninsula`; `005-map.md#spain`.

EXT. MADRID — MAY 1808

Residents confront the occupation troops in a street. People press forward from different doorways; others drag those closest to them back toward shelter. The French troops advance into the resistance.

The view is caught among residents trying to get out of the troops' path. A door is pulled almost shut, then opened again to let someone through. Shots sound in the street.

EXT. MADRID — AFTER THE SUPPRESSION

Prisoners stand before armed men. We remain with the waiting bodies until the firing starts.

Later, families search for the people taken away. A woman looks into a group of returning residents, passes them, and goes back to search among the places where the prisoners were held. Someone calls a name out of our hearing. No answer reaches us.

HISTORIAN (V.O.): Madrid resisted before Joseph came. Those who fought did not all want the same politics. They contested foreign occupation, the loss of their authority, and power over their own land. Suppression left families looking for people they would not bring home.

INT. BAYONNE — LATER IN 1808

SCREEN TEXT: Bayonne — abdications and a change of dynasty.

The formal business passes from one set of royal hands to another. The historian's voice carries us from the abdications to Napoleon facing his elder brother.

HISTORIAN (V.O.): Joseph's crown changed from Naples to Spain. The elder brother remained subject to the younger brother's imperial power.

NAPOLEON: Spain is yours to govern.

JOSEPH: With French troops keeping me there.

NAPOLEON: You will need them.

Joseph looks toward the doorway through which the business of his new government will arrive.

JOSEPH: Then every order I give will have to pass through a country that sees your soldiers first.

Napoleon turns to the next business. Joseph remains where his brother has put him.

INT. FRENCH HEADQUARTERS — LATER

A report is delivered. The man reading it reaches the name Bailén and stops to read the passage again.

HISTORIAN (V.O.): At Bailén, a French army was defeated. Reputation had failed to keep possession of Spain.

Napoleon takes the report. The room waits while he reads.

EXT. ERFURT — BEFORE NAPOLEON'S RENEWED INTERVENTION IN SPAIN

Napoleon and Alexander appear together before watching attendants. Their public courtesies continue as the historian names what the meeting actually secured.

HISTORIAN (V.O.): Russia promised to declare war on Austria if Austria declared war on France. Napoleon did not obtain the stronger commitment he sought over the placement of Russian corps. Alexander retained Russia's own judgment about entering the war.

The sovereigns part. Napoleon turns toward the men waiting on his side; Alexander is already attending to those on his.

INT. NAPOLEON'S WORKING ROOM — AFTER ERFURT

The affairs of Spain lie before Napoleon with other military business. An officer waits to know which papers to take.

NAPOLEON: I shall go to Spain.

He returns the Spanish papers separately, then keeps the remaining business before him.

NAPOLEON: These commands must be carried on without me.

The officer takes the work entrusted to him. Napoleon calls for the Spanish papers again.

EXT. IBERIAN COAST — DURING THE WAR

Cargo comes ashore from sea transport. Men carry it off while more waits aboard. The movement continues after the first load has gone inland.

HISTORIAN (V.O.): Sea supply and a base that could be sustained helped Wellington keep an army in the field.

INT. WELLINGTON'S HEADQUARTERS — DAY

Wellington listens to an officer who has come from the coast.

OFFICER: The supplies are coming ashore, sir.

WELLINGTON: Keep them moving to the army. I need to know what the base can maintain.

The officer takes his instructions away. Wellington turns to the needs still waiting before him.

EXT. PORTUGUESE AND SPANISH PREPARATIONS — SUCCESSIVE PLACES

Portuguese men prepare equipment under their own officers. Spanish groups gather and make their own preparations in inhabited country.

French supplies move under armed guard. The escort watches the road ahead and the country beside it, keeping men close to what the occupation needs carried onward.

HISTORIAN (V.O.): British, Portuguese and Spanish forces had distinct authorities. Spanish resistance made occupying the country and supplying its occupiers continuing tasks.

EXT. IBERIAN PORT — DAY

Cargo continues to pass from ship to shore as the historian reaches back to 1807.

HISTORIAN (V.O.): In 1807 the Portuguese royal family had moved to Brazil under Sidney Smith's escort. Opening Brazil's ports changed trade across the Atlantic. Smith's escort and his defense of Acre were separate achievements.

INT. JOSEPH'S GOVERNMENT — 1809

Joseph asks an official about the marshals' correspondence.

JOSEPH: Where are their reports?

OFFICIAL: They go directly to the minister of war, Sire.

JOSEPH: In France.

OFFICIAL: Yes, Sire.

Elsewhere, a marshal's dispatch is passed onward for the minister of war. It continues along that route without reaching Joseph's desk.

Back with Joseph, the government business before him requires answers.

JOSEPH: I am expected to govern with an army that reports past me.

The official cannot supply the missing reports. Joseph takes up the government business waiting on his desk.

INT. CÁDIZ — LATER IN THE CONTINUING WAR

The Cortes sits in public political work. A speaker has the floor; others attend to the business before them. Voices and the movement of people in the chamber carry through the cut to the country outside.

EXT. IBERIAN COUNTRY — THE CONTINUING WAR

French troops are still moving through a country in resistance. They pass out of sight while the distant sound of firing continues.

## Victory does not bring the capital back {#france}

Target: `docs/body/screenplay/001-chronicle/008-army-refuses.md#france`. Inherited identities: `035-france-and-paris.md#defense`, `#capital`; `008-army-refuses.md#france`.

EXT. FRANCE — CAMPAIGN, 1814

French troops move together toward the fighting. In the battle that follows, their force arrives where it can tell. The enemy gives ground. The men who have won can see it happening.

News of success reaches Napoleon.

REPORTING OFFICER: They have given ground, Sire. We have it.

Napoleon turns from the man toward the work spread before him.

NAPOLEON: Bring me the reports from the other commands.

HISTORIAN (V.O.): With fewer means, he could still concentrate men and win. The victories were real.

EXT. FRENCH ARMY — AFTER THE ENGAGEMENT

The troops move again. A man steadies himself against another as he walks. Horses pull at a burden they have to keep moving; men beside the road lift what can be carried onward.

At headquarters, the other commands' reports have arrived. Napoleon listens as an officer gives him the available strength.

NAPOLEON: If I bring them together here, what remains elsewhere?

The officer looks from the force available to the other demands on it.

OFFICER: Less than we need, Sire.

Napoleon puts a hand on the report he has been using, leaving the other requests before him unanswered.

HISTORIAN (V.O.): Men, horses, supply and time had diminished. Making strength at one point left other approaches, and Paris, harder to protect.

INT. NEGOTIATIONS AT CHÂTILLON — 1814

Caulaincourt listens to the Allied demand across the table.

ALLIED REPRESENTATIVE: France must return to the borders of 1789. Its influence outside them must be surrendered.

CAULAINCOURT: Those are not the frontiers the Emperor has authorized me to accept.

The representative waits for a different answer. Caulaincourt cannot give one.

INT. NAPOLEON'S HEADQUARTERS — DURING THE NEGOTIATIONS

Caulaincourt's warning reaches Napoleon in a dispatch. As Napoleon reads, we hear Caulaincourt's voice, belonging to the correspondence rather than to the room.

CAULAINCOURT (V.O.): They will not concede the natural frontiers because we ask again. Give me authority to secure what can still be obtained. Waiting may leave us with no negotiation at all.

Napoleon lowers the dispatch. His officer is still waiting with news of the army.

NAPOLEON: Another victory can change what they will offer.

OFFICER: And Caulaincourt's authority, Sire?

NAPOLEON: He is to continue. I will not give up the frontiers now.

The reply goes out. Napoleon turns to the military possibility still before him.

INT. ALLIED QUARTERS — THE SAME CAMPAIGN

A report of a lost engagement has been heard. The Allied representatives continue their work.

ALLIED REPRESENTATIVE: Our demands remain.

HISTORIAN (V.O.): Defeat in an engagement did not make the Allies surrender the ends for which they had entered France.

INT. NAPOLEON'S HEADQUARTERS — LATER

Napoleon returns to the correspondence from Caulaincourt.

NAPOLEON: Give Caulaincourt the authority to concede further. Send it.

The dispatch leaves him.

INT. CHÂTILLON — AFTER THE TALKS HAVE CLOSED

Caulaincourt receives the additional authority. He reads it standing beside the empty places used by the negotiators.

His hand remains on the permission while the room is cleared around him.

HISTORIAN (V.O.): The permission arrived after the talks had closed. The concessions he could now offer obtained no agreement at Châtillon in 1814.

EXT. PARIS — LATER IN THE CAMPAIGN

Allied soldiers are in the capital. Residents make room for their movement. Their marching carries us to the room where the politicians are deliberating.

INT. CAPITAL POLITICIANS' MEETING — PARIS

Talleyrand crosses to a chair, managing the difficulty in one leg. He sits among other men already discussing what government can now be maintained.

POLITICIAN: He has won battles since they entered France.

TALLEYRAND: And their armies are here.

The other man looks toward the sound of troops outside.

TALLEYRAND: We must decide what can govern Paris now.

Another politician takes up the discussion. Talleyrand turns toward him to listen.

INT. PARIS — APRIL 1, 1814

SCREEN TEXT: April 1 — a provisional government is appointed.

The men taking up the provisional government's business do so while Allied troops remain in the city.

INT. SENATE — APRIL 2, 1814

The Senate proceeds with Napoleon's deposition. A presiding voice calls the business; the men in the chamber give their answers within its formal procedure.

INT. PARIS — APRIL 3, 1814

The settled text is in the reader's hands.

HISTORIAN (V.O.): The Senate set out its accusations against Napoleon. They belonged to its case for removing him.

READER: The Emperor is deposed. His family's hereditary claim is ended. The nation and the army are released from their oaths of loyalty.

The reader goes on. Outside the chamber, Allied troops still control the streets.

EXT. NAPOLEON'S REMAINING ARMY — DAY

Soldiers wait beside their equipment. Officers pass through them carrying the army's business.

The sounds of the capital give way to voices among the soldiers and the movement of their equipment.
