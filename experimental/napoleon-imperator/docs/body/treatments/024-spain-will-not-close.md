# Changing a Dynasty Does Not End a Nation

## The change of throne proceeds despite resistance to occupation {#bayonne}

Factual parents: [Iberia](../settings/100-history/020-life-and-memory.md#expansion), [the several actors in occupation](../settings/030-society/010-power-and-cost.md#occupation), [Joseph](../settings/050-people/010-ensemble.md#joseph-bonaparte), [Wellington](../settings/050-people/010-ensemble.md#wellington), [Sidney Smith](../settings/050-people/010-ensemble.md#sidney-smith).

In Madrid, occupied by French troops in 1808, the transfer of the royal family to Bayonne meets resistance from citizens. Suppression with executions comes first; the abdications at Bayonne and the crown to be given Joseph follow. Do not reverse the resistance already present into a reaction to the arrival of a new king. Military force may quiet the streets for the moment, but cannot create the consent of residents and other powers.

Joseph's throne changes from Naples to Spain, but his rank as eldest brother does not change the political superiority of his younger brother the Emperor. The country he is to govern depends on the presence of French troops, and that presence itself gives reason for resistance. His interest in establishing his own government diverges from the need to conduct his brother's war. Do not briefly use violence suffered by residents as an illustration explaining the Emperor's bad reputation. Those resisting act to protect the land in which they live and their own authority; those coercing create more enemies in the name of restoring order. The audience loses the earlier expectation that sending an army completes the work of government.

## Defeat and renewed intervention leave the same problem {#bailen}

The French defeat at Bailén separates a reputation for invincibility from actual capacity to occupy. Do not combine the purposes of residents, guerrillas, and other Spanish political forces into one unified command. Joseph's kingship inherits a state in which it needs the French army but loses his country's consent because of the war that army fights.

Later at Erfurt Napoleon reaffirms the Tilsit alliance with Alexander. He obtains a promise that if Austria declares war on France, Russia will declare war on Austria too, but the stronger constraint he wanted, that he judge when Russia should enter the war and Russian corps be stationed near the Austrian border, does not remain in the final agreement. Give the audience both the public relationship of the monarchs and the gains and limits of this secret agreement, without explaining the difference through unrecorded words at the meeting.

Without securing all of Russia's actions, Napoleon leaves this limited agreement in place and heads for Spain. Direct intervention is not a reward that removes every danger on other fronts, but a choice to use battlefield strength again while bearing remaining risks and the burden of delegation. Even that strength cannot create the consent of the whole country at once. The more he uses his talent on one battlefield, the more he must entrust other places to others.

In 1809, while war and government continue in Spain, instructions that marshals correspond with the minister of war without going through Joseph put a real limit on his kingship. Joseph seeks to govern through the authority of a local government, but the flow of military command bypasses him. The right to his own kingdom he had desired in Naples returns as a contradiction: even the army needed to defend his country does not gather under his command. The marshals' bypass is not the cause of all Spanish resistance; the mismatch leaves a burden of separately managing government and war in what follows.

The war in Iberia continues on the prior condition that the Portuguese royal family has already moved to Brazil. Give as brief historical background the condition that this move and the opening of ports changed relations across the sea, and that Sidney Smith's 1807 escort was an achievement separate from Acre; do not return the scene to that year. Wellington needs not luck in fighting while avoiding the Emperor, but supplies, maritime connections, and conditions to sustain the distinct forces of Britain, Portugal, and Spain.

While France cannot eliminate all resistance, its opponents can continue the war. Spain's royal family, the Cortes at Cádiz, guerrillas, and the Allies do not share one authority and objective simply because they oppose Napoleon. This front does not close as a victory when the main narrative moves to the next Austrian war. It remains an open burden tying down imperial manpower and command.
