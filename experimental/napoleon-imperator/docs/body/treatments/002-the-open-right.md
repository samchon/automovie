# The Vacated Heights and Narrowing Choices

## Retreat buys time to prepare {#withdrawal}

The retreat through the night of November 28–29 and the withdrawal from Austerlitz on the 29th change the French line. For the troops leaving, this is an actual abandonment of ground, not a performance of a victory they already know. Napoleon gains time to bring his dispersed army together while vacating the Pratzen and making his right appear weak. Between the orders Berthier conveys and the corps' actual movement lies a road still to be walked.

A paper ordering concentration does not complete it. Bernadotte arrives within the range November 30–December 1; Friant's troops reach Raygern on the night of the 1st, only then readying their next movement. The audience follows the gaps being filled, but not every soldier knows the Emperor's entire intention in advance. Where accounts differ over the exact start of withdrawal, do not reconcile them into a single time.

## The other side has reasons to expect victory too {#allied-plan}

In the Allied command, Alexander wants victory and the initiative, while Kutuzov's nominal command is not the same as actual influence beside the sovereign. Weyrother's attack plan aims to turn the French right and cut the road to Vienna. Reading the apparently weak French as an opportunity is not simple stupidity: the enemy's disposition meets the Allies' own objective.

Reveal both the advantage of moving south and the danger of emptying the center. The hope that a French envoy's moderate manner will be read as fear remains a French interpretation. Do not make one mission an act of control over the enemy's entire mind. Once the plan is divided into orders and sent down to the army, the Allies too are constrained by columns, roads, and delays in transmission. Both sides' choices now become movements difficult to reverse.
