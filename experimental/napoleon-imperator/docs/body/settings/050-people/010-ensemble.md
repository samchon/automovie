French tu and vous are not mechanically interchangeable with fixed English pronouns.

Do not say law forced all French people to use familiar speech during the Revolution. Distinguish citizens' forms of address and attempts to erase distance in party and office from relationships in general society. Do not turn foreign accents into English regional dialects. Dialogue outside the table follows the individual's original voice and authority. Use the source `020-writing-rules#name-forms` and individual H2 titles as reference while rendering names consistently in conventional English or French forms. Francis is “Francis II” in 1805 and “Francis I” after August 6, 1806. Do not merge different people under one shortened name, such as Auguste de Caulaincourt and his older brother, or valet Louis Marchand and General Jean-Gabriel Marchand.

Nicknames have dates of origin. Do not bring forward Ney's “bravest of the brave” as his own description before 1812 or Davout's Iron Marshal as a fixed title before 1806. “The Eaglet” is a later name from a work in 1835, not what the son called himself in life. Catherine Hubscher's “Madame Sans-Gêne” is the name of an 1893 play. The verb for betrayal derived from Marmont is also reception after 1814. Place these later titles beside earlier events only when the historian reveals their formation.
