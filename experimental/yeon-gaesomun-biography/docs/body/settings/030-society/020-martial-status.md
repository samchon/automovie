## Recognition through military achievement and masculine honor {#martial-status}

**Definition and scope**

In the work’s Goguryeo, military achievement is the quickest route to recognition beyond birth limits. Nobles support authority through achievements leading troops; commoners’ performance changes superiors’ trust, rewards, and next mobilization duties. Higher office also depends on family troops and wealth, marriage ties, and recommendation; achievement alone does not immediately secure a desired post. Still, a person born poor who is strong and fights well can rise in rank. [Ondal](../040-agents/084-ondal.md#ondal), who begged to support his mother, is remembered as an example: first in military achievement, he received daehyeong and became the king’s son-in-law.

**Established facts**

Nobi status can arise from unpaid debts or crime. The Book of Zhou says children were made nobi to repay compensation or public and private debts. The work does not portray status as hereditary across generations: children of nobi are not born nobi, and people who repay debts return to commoner status. Records do not establish heritability; noninheritance is the work’s choice.

Men skilled in martial arts and strong in body find it easy to gain face. Young people belittle companions poor at archery or weak in public, and may even interpret a call to endure a long war as fear. These judgments are pressure people apply to one another, not values the narrator endorses. Responses to an injured veteran unable to draw a bow also vary, shaped by memory of earlier service and community contribution and his care of family and livelihood.

Such pressure helps a country fight but leaves temptation after victory to divide power by force. A person with great achievements thinks he has earned the right to govern; someone who guarded home and field feels the burden borne without a chance at achievement goes unrecognized. In the succession civil war these demands for recognition are consumed in fighting.
