## The foundation built by Pyeongwon and Yeongyang {#first-restoration}

**Definition and scope**

Pyeongwon and Yeongyang gather people scattered after civil war and reconnect cooperation needed for production and military mobilization. Their reigns form the work’s first reconstruction. They judge they lack resources both to cross all southern fortresses and sustain conquest and to fight continuously against Northern Zhou and Sui growing stronger in the west. They expand the foundation through trade, protection, and education with eastern Malgal communities. Detailed systematic development and Gyeongdang policies are constructed for that choice.

**Established facts**

In the west real military consolidation is underway. Northern Zhou, once one state among several, unites Northern Qi’s large forces, commanderies, and population when Emperor Wu conquers it in 577. Yang Jian, a Northern Zhou commander, founds Sui and conquers Chen in 589, extending the foundation into a unified dynasty’s power southward. Both rulers are outstanding in war and government and lay foundations for Chinese reunification. Distinguish Northern Zhou Wu’s era from the Sixteen Kingdoms. Goguryeo’s earlier kings accept western recovery as unavoidable and choose eastward expansion.

Reconstruction needs families to answer mobilization again and households to retain seed for next year. It also takes time for trading communities to profit more from living together than raiding. Recovery requires family, household, and trading-community cooperation along with central orders. The kings seek room to continue recovery amid military threats. Watching this, Go Geonmu learns that royal authority is built both by a battlefield victory and years of work keeping promises.

Ondal’s achievement against Northern Zhou shows the background for recruiting talent of common origin during reconstruction. His biography says he took dozens of heads in the vanguard and the army gained momentum. The work adds his Gyeongdang upbringing and marriage’s political significance, without making him single-handedly complete national reconstruction. Northern Zhou aiming for Liaoxi and Liaodong is the work’s geographical and objective reconstruction. Scholars disagree on the encounter, opponent, and location; the cited record alone does not establish that goal.
