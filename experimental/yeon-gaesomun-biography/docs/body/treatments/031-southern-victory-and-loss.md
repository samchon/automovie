## The result at Baekgang arrives in autumn {#baekgang-report-arrives}

In autumn 663, definite news of the Baekje restoration forces' collapse reaches Pyongyang. It forces them to abandon the expectation that a Goguryeo victory would improve the southern war too. The report gives the internal conflict in which Buyeo Pung killed Boksin in the sixth lunar month, the naval defeat at Baekgang in the eighth lunar month, and the loss of land strongholds in that order. Do not conflate them by having Boksin command at Baekgang. Wa's support had begun before news of Goguryeo's survival; after that survival, already assembled forces and the crisis at Juryuseong kept the relief war going.

Pyongyang receives news explaining the defeat, not a reenactment of the battlefield. Namsaeng and Gaesomun read the causes: reinforcements behind could not correct the confusion of the leading ships on the water, further commitments increased losses while people and ships were being rescued, and the fall of land strongholds removed the ability to recover. The roughly 30,000 in Wa's main supporting force and the record of 27,000 dispatched in the third lunar month are different figures. Do not calculate the reported scale as everyone present in one place on the day of Baekgang.

Buyeo Pung's flight to Goguryeo leaves it with the burden of receiving an exile. His marriage in Wa and ties between the ruling classes of the two countries are used only as reports explaining the strength he could mobilize and the relationships he lost. Whether his spouse accompanied him or survived, and any new Wa war plan, remain undisclosed. The narrator does not reduce Baekje's loss to incidental news for victorious Goguryeo, but returns to Pyongyang's policy response without opening a separate Wa campaign.

The report also says that after Juryuseong surrendered, Baekje people discussed routes for leaving with the Wa side and told their families of their intention to relocate. The lament for the loss of their country's name and the way back to their ancestors' graves belongs to the local Baekje people. The narrator notes that Wa's support was a relationship involving more than military supplies, without turning their words into proof of the ancestry of Wa's entire ruling class ([the people behind the support and relocation](../settings/040-agents/073-yamato-support.md#yamato-support)). The news adds weight to the question of what future the surviving country in Pyongyang must now prepare.

## Different readings of a stopped war and a growing south {#different-readings-of-the-south}

Report Tang's abolition of ship construction and declaration ending the war on day 27 of the eighth lunar month of 663 separately from news of the Baekgang collapse. Gaesomun recognizes the achievement of defeating the elite army in the field and thereby actually stopping the policy of conquest. He does not conclude that Baekgang and Tang's cessation of war against Goguryeo were the same decision, or that hostilities have disappeared forever. There is more reason to devote effort to return and reconstruction.

The historian separately reports the outcome that Silla, too, abandoned a large-scale postwar offensive to conquer Goguryeo. This does not mean local clashes and the later southern threat disappeared.

Namsaeng judges that the disappearance of Baekje's major resistance, which had occupied Silla, is allowing a base of people, grain, and transport larger than Goguryeo's to form in the south. He does not claim that all Baekje's resources immediately became Silla's. He acknowledges Tang's garrisons, remaining resistance, and divided rule, but says he has already seen Silla's ability to bring them together. Having experienced Kim Yusin's winter transport, he takes the report less as a distant possibility than as pressure to accelerate reconstruction.

Namgeon and Namsan draw the opposite conclusion from the same incomplete integration. Namgeon demands that they pressure Silla before dispersing the army that defeated Tang; Namsan believes royal and central support can turn the offensive into a national undertaking. Namsaeng settles on maintaining defensive troops while restoring production and roads first, without expanding an expeditionary force. Gaesomun supports the decision, so no immediate offensive opens, but the younger brothers feel their experience is treated as less important than their elder brother's defeat. In the reconstruction that follows, policy results and opposition to their distribution grow together.
