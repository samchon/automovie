# A father who believes his work is finished

## Gaesomun sees the country he will leave his son {#yeon-accepts-the-restoration-plan}

In spring 665, before a weakened Gaesomun, Namsaeng explains both the recovery under way and the work still delayed. This is not a report of a completed revival. What Gaesomun can see now is the decision to delay construction for sowing, the choice to retain defenders without expanding foreign expeditions, and the achievement of dividing work through cooperation in Liaodong and Gungnaeseong. He accepts that his son's way of ruling, unlike his own concentration of every task in one hand, may be sounder for the next era.

He also sees the belonging of Malgal communities as a real achievement. Households that received support ask for conditions again, and light cavalry undertook important missions as the defense of their own country; these are not evidence of loosened subjection. Gaesomun reads a continuity between the foundation expanded by two kings, the protection he delivered, and their shared victory. Do not extend that into a declaration that all Malgal are on the same side or a guarantee of unconditional future obedience to the Yeon family.

On the strength of these achievements, Gaesomun privately judges that he has repaid his debt to the king he killed. He is satisfied that he first stopped the invasion and raised a son who shares the king's desire for recovery. Do not make him confess that judgment to Namsaeng or turn it into the dead king's approval. The narrator holds together his conviction that the killing was necessary and the real opportunity for recovery gained, without using a collapse that has not yet happened to make his satisfaction a false achievement.

## He asks for unity and dies without seeing the future {#unity-advice-and-death}

Even as he hands over work, Gaesomun cannot let go of several final decisions. He wants to live longer to see the country his son creates, but does not try to turn back time by increasing his own military duties again. He fears that the royal house and nobles he held down may expand their power again under the mild Namsaeng. He does not know who will join whom, when a coup will come, or that his own sons will fight a civil war with those forces.

He urges his three sons to unite, using the lesson that bundled branches are difficult to break. They accept the advice, but do not renegotiate which policies and powers they agree on. The admonition secures obedience in the moment; it cannot substitute for a mechanism of cooperation after his death. The anecdote is an action chosen by the work, not wording to quote as a confirmed historical statement. Namsaeng takes it to mean he can keep the charge by continuing to use his brothers' abilities. Namgeon places unity in an offensive continuing his father's victory; Namsan places it in cooperation between the royal house and family. All three receive the same advice while expecting different ways to carry it out.

Gaesomun dies believing that he has completed his life's work and left the conditions for revival. Namsaeng succeeds as daemangniji and takes final responsibility for reconstruction, while Namgeon and Namsan continue their central duties. The death and succession become known to domestic families, the royal house, and central commanders, and reach the regions as news travels. Do not transition to Tang knowing the death and succession with certainty at the same moment. The father's life closes here; those remaining inherit both a country actually entrusted to them and relationships of power on which they have not agreed.
