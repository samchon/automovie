# The era and reconstruction

## National collapse before reconstruction {#earlier-civil-war-and-collapse}

Reconstruct the mid-sixth-century succession civil war and disorder, beginning late in King Anwon's reign in 545 and continuing under King Yangwon (545–559), as an event killing 2,000 nobles alone and collapsing government and defense. In the north the Turks take territory and influence; in the south a Silla–Baekje coalition takes territory, and some nobles flee to Silla and Baekje. The dynasty survives, but the crisis requires rebuilding a country effectively ruined.

This establishes a precedent in which a society contesting power through force destroys its own ruling class and national foundations. Making every dead person a noble, and the specific extent of northern losses and flight, are the work's reconstruction.

- [National Institute of Korean History, division and conflict among noble forces](https://contents.history.go.kr/front/hm/view.do?levelId=hm_009_0070): records the Chugun–Segun succession dispute and more than 2,000 deaths, without establishing every victim's status.
- [National Institute of Korean History, notes on King Yangwon's accession](https://db.history.go.kr/item/compareViewer.do?levelId=sg_019r_0050_0010): explains Turkic pressure and loss of the Han River basin to the Silla–Baekje coalition in 551.

## Two reconstructions {#two-restorations}

Depict Kings Pyeongwon and Yeongyang as restoring the country broken by civil war through long effort and sacrifice, creating the strength to defeat Sui's invasion. After victory in the Sui wars, Go Geonmu inherits the burden of rebuilding a country devastated again. Treat victory and national exhaustion together.

The same national task continues into Geonmu's cautious policy and later Namsaeng's reconstruction vision. The intensity of framing the period as two near-collapses and recoveries is the work's chosen view.

## Repeated invasions and memories of war {#repeated-invasions-and-war-memory}

In Goguryeo experience, treat the Sui wars as repeated disappointment of the expectation that defeating a major invasion will bring respect and peace. After the 598 war and diplomatic settlement comes hope that war is over, but in 612 another great army claiming two million arrives. Hopes that Sui's internal rebellions will stop expeditions, or that a great defeat means it will never return, collapse with renewed invasions in 613 and 614. Let contemporary characters perceive the threat of Sui's claimed two million through uncertain reports; distinguish the recorded 1,133,800 in later narration and commentary as a figure in the record. Do not give everyone the same news simultaneously.

Invasions continuing even as the enemy country collapses through rebellion and defeat leave fear that Goguryeo cannot recover daily life even when it wins. Geonmu's generation guards against the strength and expeditionary persistence of a unified Central Plain dynasty, while ordinary people seek to avoid livelihoods collapsing again under prolonged scorched-earth evacuation and siege. Preserve the causality by which the same memory produces cautious diplomacy and defense on one hand and demands for a short decisive war on the other. Settings determines specific people, information routes, and scenes through which expectations and fears change.

Treat Sui's final 614 expedition as a point when both countries struggled to continue war. Sui's levied troops fail to assemble in time; after defeat in fighting toward Bisaseong, Goguryeo asks to surrender and returns Gok Sajeong. Emperor Yang accepts and orders withdrawal. Also use the record that Lai Hu'er urged continued attack citing Goguryeo's exhaustion. Interpret Goguryeo's surrender request as a choice to end war without further exhausting land and livelihoods, and use it as background to later popular preference for a short decision over prolonged siege.

From the same memory of victory arise both vigilance against invasion and a desire to avoid a long war. The changing expectations and fears are the work's interpretation.

- [National Institute of Korean History, Sui's 598 withdrawal and restoration of relations](https://db.history.go.kr/ancient/level.do?levelId=sg_020r_0020_0110): records withdrawal, Goguryeo's apology, and restoration of the prior relationship. Characters' expectation of peace afterward is the work's interpretation.
- [National Institute of Korean History, Sui's 612 army](https://db.history.go.kr/ancient/level.do?levelId=sg_020_0020_0220): gives both the recorded 1,133,800 and the claimed two million. The work constructs what information reaches Goguryeo people.
- [National Institute of Korean History, the 613 expedition and Yang Xuangan's rebellion](https://db.history.go.kr/id/sg_020r_0020_0330): records renewed invasion, crisis at Yodongseong, rebellion, and Gok Sajeong's flight to Goguryeo.
- [National Institute of Korean History, Yeongyang's surrender request in his 25th year](https://db.history.go.kr/ancient/level.do?levelId=sg_020r_0020_0360): records Sui's mobilization difficulties, Goguryeo's exhaustion, defeat toward Bisaseong, surrender request, Gok Sajeong's return, and withdrawal order. Distinguish this from a statement that Bisaseong itself fell.
- [Book of Sui, volume 64, biography of Lai Hu'er](https://angelibrary.com/oldies/duoshu/064.htm): records Lai's argument to continue toward Pyongyang, citing Goguryeo's exhaustion and fields without green grass, followed by his obedience to the withdrawal order. The connection to popular demands for short decisive war is the work's reconstruction.

## An eastern foundation for recovery {#eastern-restoration-base}

During Pyeongwon and Yeongyang's reigns, Silla's southern fortress network and growing Northern Zhou and Sui strength in the west limit expansion. Emphasize Emperor Wu of Northern Zhou and Emperor Wen of Sui as capable rulers who laid foundations for reunification of China. Reconstruct Northern Zhou's attack as directed at Liaoxi and Liaodong. The two Goguryeo kings incorporate and develop eastern Malgal areas, widening the base of population, production, supply, and mobilization. Connect education and belonging to [Malgal incorporation](005-malgal-and-belonging.md#integration-and-education). Counting the east, the sphere of influence may have reached its widest area under Yeongyang; having lost the Han River basin, however, its practical value was not necessarily greatest. The narrator states both aspects.

Show recovery of national strength through incorporating people and regions into its foundation as well as battlefield victory. Settings designs development and economic institutions. Emphasizing Yeongyang's area over the conventional view of the late fifth century as widest is the work's perspective.

- [National Institute of Korean History, Ondal's service against Northern Zhou](https://db.history.go.kr/id/sg_045r_0100_0150): records Emperor Wu's attack and Goguryeo victory; notes introduce differing views of the encounter and location. Liaoxi and Liaodong as objectives are the work's choice.

## Turkic power changes international conditions {#turkic-power-and-tang}

Treat the Turks as a major force changing rivalry between Northern Zhou and Northern Qi, Goguryeo–Sui relations, and conditions for Tang's founding and eastern expeditions. Give weight to Tang's concentration east after subduing northern nomadic powers, and Turkic commanders and troops incorporated into Tang participating in campaigns against Goguryeo. In the second war, reflect Qibi Heli, Xiao Siye, and others leaving the front to address a Tiele rebellion as operational conditions. Later Turkic revival changing international conditions again is preserved side-story material, not a production order for this main work. Place Khitan as a northwestern force changing affiliation with northern power. Some Khitan driven out by Turks shelter with Goguryeo, then depart for Sui and Tang; in 648 the Khitan Dahe group submits to Tang and the Songmo commandery is established. In 654 Goguryeo attacks Khitan to regain influence but loses at Sinseong.

This lets confrontation between China and Goguryeo move with northern alliances, subordination, and division. Distinguish Turkic and Tiele groups according to their affiliations and interests in each period.

- [Northeast Asian History Foundation, Goguryeo's foreign relations under Emperor Yang of Sui](https://contents.nahf.or.kr/item/level.do?levelId=gt.d_0006_0010_0020_0020): explains Turkic international standing and Goguryeo–Sui negotiation.
- [Northeast Asian History Foundation, Goguryeo's counterattack and Tang withdrawal](https://contents.nahf.or.kr/item/level.do?levelId=gt.d_0007_0010_0020_0020): connects some Tang forces' departure to address the Tiele rebellion with the crisis on the Pyongyang front.
- [Book of Sui, volume 84, account of the Khitan](https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/隋書/卷84): records 10,000 Khitan households driven by Turks sheltering with Goguryeo and later Chulbok and others of a separate group betraying Goguryeo and submitting to Sui.

## Silla and Baekje revive; the south changes {#southern-revival-and-resource-balance}

Treat Silla as a country that expanded greatly after a period under Goguryeo influence. Reconstruct it as fearing southward expansion by a revived Goguryeo, using [existing fortresses and northern strongholds](#silla-fortresses-and-limits-of-southern-campaigns) and strengthening its network. Baekje passes through crisis after King Seong's death and restores an offensive under Kings Mu and Uija; Silla pays for underestimating its revival. Supplement Baekje's detailed background according to verified records and research.

Treat the Baekje restoration movement as 660–663, overlapping the second Goguryeo–Tang war before its principal forces collapse. Adopt a long-term balance in which Goguryeo has the population and economic advantage over Silla while Silla and Baekje fight separately, but falls behind if Baekje's base is integrated into Silla. Integration does not occur all at once immediately after the fall. Connect restoration Baekje and Wa's motives for preparing war to [Baekgang and national survival](#baekgang-and-national-survival).

Neighboring recovery and integration change Goguryeo's future choices. Relative national strength is the work's structure; do not present ancient population and economic totals as established figures.

- [National Institute of Korean History, King Mu](https://contents.history.go.kr/mobile/kc/view.do?code=kc_age_10&levelId=kc_n101005): explains royal consolidation and offensives against Silla after defeat at Amakseong.
- [National Institute of Korean History, Baekje restoration movement](https://contents.history.go.kr/front/kc/printViewPopup.do?levelId=kc_i101020): explains 660–663 resistance, internal conflict, war with the Silla–Tang coalition, and failure.

## Silla's fortress network and the limits of southern campaigns {#silla-fortresses-and-limits-of-southern-campaigns}

Depict Silla's defense as a system relying on mountain fortresses to guard chokepoints, crossings, and military and supply bases in occupied areas, connecting them with relief armies. Use Samnyeonsanseong as an existing strategic stronghold built in 470 and repaired in 486, not a fortress newly built under Pyeongwon to block Goguryeo. Major reference points include Danyang Jeokseong toward Jungnyeong and the Namhan River, Chiljungseong controlling the Imjin crossing and southward route, and the contemporary Bukhansanseong that held against Goguryeo attacks in 603 and 661. Do not merge them into a single straight defensive line or facilities built at the same time for the same purpose.

Explain Ondal's death through the risk of breaking such defenses. Individual valor alone cannot solve walls, difficult ground, and a garrison's response; even after taking one fortress, troops are needed for the next attack and to hold supply lines and occupied ground. Later Goguryeo repeatedly strikes Silla's openings, but lacks capacity to sustain large-scale conquest and cannot expand local gains into conquest of all Silla. Preserve actual successes such as the coalition's capture of 33 fortresses in 655. The limit is not failure of every attack, but the burden of conquest and retention even after gaining positions.

Ground Ondal's death and later southern constraints in Silla's defensive ability and Goguryeo's expeditionary capacity. Settings examines and designs individual fortress roles and occupation periods, and expedition size and routes; retain the location debate over Adanseong, where Ondal died. Do not transfer present walls and later facilities directly into the period.

- [Encyclopedia of Korean Culture, Samnyeonsanseong](https://encykorea.aks.ac.kr/Article/E0026508): explains its 470 construction, 486 repair, and strategic role in Silla's northern expansion and unification wars.
- [Encyclopedia of Korean Culture, Danyang Silla Jeokseong stele](https://encykorea.aks.ac.kr/Article/E0013624): describes Jeokseong at the Jungnyeong and Namhan River approaches as a King Jinheung-era forward base for campaigns against Goguryeo.
- [Gyeonggi Cultural Heritage Institute, Paju Chiljungseong](https://gjicp.ggcf.kr/mediaObjects/573): explains its position controlling the Imjin crossing and southern route, and repeated occupations and reconstruction.
- [Encyclopedia of Korean Culture, battle of Chiljungseong](https://encykorea.aks.ac.kr/Article/E0071954): describes Alcheon's 638 defeat of Goguryeo troops outside the fortress. Fortress defense can include field response supported by the fortress.
- [Lee Sang-hun, “The Battle of Bukhansanseong in 661 and Kim Yusin's Response,” 2016](https://www.kci.go.kr/kciportal/landing/article.kci?arti_id=ART002188147): the public abstract explains relief arrival in 603 and attack and withdrawal in 661, interpreting additional catapults in the latter. Its detailed interpretation is not all adopted.
- [National Institute of Korean History, capture of 33 fortresses on Silla's northern frontier in 655](https://db.history.go.kr/common/compareViewer.do?levelId=sg_005_0040_0080&type=ancient): records their capture by a Goguryeo–Baekje–Malgal coalition. Do not turn it into a Goguryeo-only achievement.

## Southern change after victory presses reconstruction {#postwar-reconstruction-and-southern-threat}

Victory in the second Goguryeo–Tang war and Tang's cessation of conquest give Goguryeo time to recover, but the 663 collapse of Baekje restoration forces creates a different anxiety: a southern check has disappeared and Silla's strength may grow. Together with Silla's capacity demonstrated by Kim Yusin's winter provision transport, this gives Yeon Gaesomun and Namsaeng reason to restore land, livelihoods, production, and defenses instead of immediately beginning a war of conquest.

Depict postwar reconstruction as a revival building strength to endure even if war returns. Tang's abandonment of conquest is a real achievement, but tension remains that without using the time, Goguryeo cannot withstand the changed southern balance and future war. Do not predetermine the next opponent, timing, or choice between attack and defense.

Judgments taking victory as present security and the restoration forces' fall as future danger both support reconstruction. Their causal effect on Goguryeo leadership policy is the work's reconstruction; settings designs report timing and individual responses.

- [National Institute of Korean History, Baekje restoration movement](https://contents.history.go.kr/front/kc/printViewPopup.do?levelId=kc_i101020): explains the collapse of major restoration forces in 663. It does not directly prove a judgment leading to Goguryeo reconstruction.

## Baekgang and national survival {#baekgang-and-national-survival}

Let the Baekje restoration army know of the 661–662 large-scale Goguryeo–Tang offensive and fear that if Goguryeo falls, Tang's forces will turn on them and eliminate their opportunity for restoration. They judge that survival against Tang requires destroying Silla in a short decisive war to gain its territory, population, and productive base, and their urgency to fight even without adequate preparation drives great risk. Wa prepares and dispatches a large supporting force amid ties between the two ruling classes and concern about Tang expansion. News that Goguryeo survived changes their initial premise, but support already requested and forces already mobilized remain. In 663 the crisis at Juryuseong prompts commitment of those forces to the Baekgang relief battle. This is the work's interpretation, not an established recorded motive for the actual dispatch.

Treat Baekgang and its associated land campaign as a war for survival between Silla and restoration Baekje. Let Silla understand and respond to the threat the restoration army and Wa pose to its country. Use roughly 30,000 for Wa's main support, distinct from the record of 27,000 dispatched in March 663; settings determines troops, fleet, and preparation from earlier support through Baekgang.

The crisis on Goguryeo's front creates urgency for the restoration army before preparations are complete, and each country's war continues after Goguryeo's victory. The prospect of Goguryeo's fall, fear of Tang forces returning, and causality linking destruction of Silla to resources and a rash decisive battle are the work's reconstruction, not a source-confirmed joint strategy. Settings designs the operation's specific overreach and judgments.

- [Nihon Shoki, volume 27, Emperor Tenji](https://www.seisaku.bz/nihonshoki/shoki_27.html): records 27,000 dispatched to attack Silla in March 663 and defeat at Baekgang that year. Goguryeo's anticipated fall as a motive is the work's reconstruction.
- [National Institute of Korean History, the battle of Baekgang](https://contents.history.go.kr/front/tg/view.do?levelId=tg_001_0150&treeId=0103): explains the 663 battle between restoration Baekje and Wa and the Silla–Tang coalition, Juryuseong's fall after defeat, and the movement's collapse.

## Ruling classes connected across the border {#wa-baekje-ties-and-mobilization}

Baekje and Wa are separate countries, but depict their ruling classes as deeply connected through royal stays and returns, marriages, and migration and exchange among nobles and clans. Those ties and concern over Tang expansion both contribute to Wa's relief of Baekje. For some, Baekje's fall also means loss of kin and a long-connected community. Settings determines specific families and relationships.

Treat the restoration movement's failure as loss of both the country and the route back to ancestral graves. Use the Nihon Shoki's lament by Baekje people and exile toward Wa to show cross-border relationships shaping people's paths after defeat.

Show closeness through royal and noble exchange and choices of relief and exile, without expanding debates in Baekje history into a separate plot. Do not settle the ancestry or everyday language of all Wa's ruling class or the etymology of 'Kudara.' If explanation is needed, briefly distinguish the name's existence, linguistic and written exchange, and hypotheses about language family and range of use.

Ground Wa's intervention in relationships and interests across the sea. Interpretations of the relationship differ; do not make closeness an identical perception among all members. The lament for ancestral graves is recorded as Baekje people's speech, not proof of the ancestry of Wa's entire ruling class.

- [Nihon Shoki, volume 27, Emperor Tenji](https://www.seisaku.bz/nihonshoki/shoki_27.html): records Pungjang's marriage and return to Baekje, and Baekje people's lament for graves and movement toward Wa after Juryuseong's surrender in September 663. The work connects individual family relationships and relief motives.
- [Na Haeng-ju, “Reasons for Dispatch and Defeat in the Baekchon River War,” 2023](https://www.kci.go.kr/kciportal/landing/article.kci?arti_id=ART003022600): the public abstract identifies kinship through long royal diplomacy and shared political culture as major motives. The threat of Tang as a dispatch motive is the work's reconstruction.
- [National Diet Library reference database, origin of 'Kudara'](https://crd.ndl.go.jp/reference/entry/index.php?id=1000086969&page=ref_view): introduces the Japanese name and several etymologies. The name alone cannot establish the ruling classes' everyday language.
- [Ito Hideto, 「朝鮮半島における言語接触」, 2013](https://www.tufs.ac.jp/common/fs/ilr/contents/ronshuu/18/jilr_18_note_Ito.pdf): pages 64–65 discuss mixed linguistic groups and court language, interpreting use of Wa language at the Wa court from Book of Sui terminology. Distinguish the existence of Baekje-related language from the everyday language of the whole court.

## Limits on Tang after Goguryeo's fall {#limits-of-tang-conquest}

Tang destroys Goguryeo but fails to rule the entire Korean peninsula because of resistance by former residents, war with Silla, and Tibetan offensives. Its later acceptance of Silla's and Balhae's survival involves wars and diplomacy in different periods. The main work covers conditions only through 679; extending to Balhae's survival remains a preserved possibility for a separate side story.

Retain both success in conquering Goguryeo and frustration of Tang's overall conquest goal, continuing the pattern that victory guarantees no entire future.

- [National Institute of Korean History, the Silla–Tang war](https://contents.history.go.kr/mobile/kc/view.do?levelId=kc_i101220): explains Tang's intention to rule the peninsula, Silla's response, the threat on the Tibetan front, and Tang's strategic change.
