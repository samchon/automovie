# Script population obligations

The completed script population answers once for its partition, titles, material allocation, and executable handoff.

## Does the chosen partition give the audience meaningful units? {#partition-and-titles}

Place act, part, sequence, episode, or season boundaries according to the actual form, where the delivered unit changes something and leaves an earned reason to continue or stop. A quota of scenes or a cut through an unfinished exchange cannot establish a useful boundary. On return after a release interval, provide the action, goals, address, objects, or other retrieval cues needed to follow the current choice without requiring a recap. Avoid defaulting every unit to the same crisis. Where a declared purchase boundary exists, inspect whether the preceding material has already delivered the work's promise and reward.

Compare audience-facing titles with each unit's actual content and prior viewing. The promised event, object, conflict, image, or question must gain meaning there; interchangeable titles or absent promises fail. Numbers, ambiguous or poetic titles, repeated titles with part numbers, and deliberate refrains can function without explaining the plot or spoiling a reveal. Preserve user-fixed wording. Script titles and identities pass to descendants; repair semantic mismatches here rather than quietly renaming final units.

## Does every assigned event become a complete executable handoff? {#complete-execution}

Map every treatment to its implementing scripts and every script to its actual parents, allowing honest many-to-many relations while exposing missing, orphaned, and duplicated progression. Compare each partition's assigned execution material with the declared scale: no essential invention, repetition, or padding may be required later to sustain it. A script-to-screenplay text-length ratio cannot decide sufficiency. Trace the cause to the boundary, event assignment, or delivery promise and repair its owner.

Read every H2 in each file as an independent initial execution script. Can a filmmaker stage its essential action, process, exchange, transition, consequence, appearance, blocking, light, in-scene sound, atmosphere, viewing position, and meaning-changing delivery without inventing them? A treatment summary, report of elapsed action, dialogue buried in explanation, or named result without its intervening execution fails even if a specialist could later rescue it. Leave composition, lens values, incidental acting detail, and other finishing choices open only where they do not replace script-owned decisions. Final screenplay adds its audiovisual realization to this already executable handoff.
