# Fight coverage

The owner judged an earlier cut's camera static. This study records how fights are shot for broadcast and film, the law the film's camera follows and the measurable targets it must meet.

## Broadcast and film practice {#practice}

Broadcast boxing centres on a fixed hard camera with the broadest view of the ring. Ringside handheld cameras move with the action for closer sizes, and a knockdown is the moment to change angle ([Fightful: wrestling and combat camera roles](https://www.fightful.com/?p=333755), [SVG: Wilder–Fury II production](https://www.sportsvideo.org/2020/02/21/wilder-vs-fury-2-espn-fox-sports-team-up-on-outsize-boxing-production-in-vegas), [DVinfo: shooting boxing](https://www.dvinfo.net/forum/open-dv-discussion/465615-shooting-boxing.html)).

Fiction films took two routes. *Raging Bull* keeps the camera inside the ring and moving with the fighters ([No Film School](https://nofilmschool.com/raging-bull-fight-scenes)). *Creed* stages a whole fight as one moving take that changes from mid-shot to close-up without cutting ([Adorama: Creed and the evolution of the fight scene](https://www.adorama.com/alc/shooting-holiday-blockbuster-creed-and-evolution-fight-scene/)).

Bordwell's survey of intensified continuity gives action films average shot lengths near 2.7 s, against 8–11 s in the studio era ([Intensified continuity](https://www.scribd.com/doc/310344009/Bordwell-Intensified-Continuity)).

## Law {#law}

One continuous inside-the-ring take, as in *Creed*. The camera keeps a side view of both fighters from across their line, on the side opposite the referee, so he never blocks the action. Its size follows the action: wide while the fighters reset, medium while an exchange is live, and a push-in on a clean landing, each size held at least 2.4 s, just under the 2.7 s action average, so a size reads before it changes. It orbits instead of cutting through the fighters, at no more than 0.9 rad/s (film choice: a quarter turn in under two seconds, the pace of an operator walking the ring's edge), and follows with an operator's lag. At a knockdown it widens to hold the fallen fighter, the referee and the standing fighter together. The finishing blow is filmed from its open side: the side of the attacker–receiver line where the struck region lies, so neither man's body stands between the lens and the striking surface, the contact and the leg's descent. If a body still blocks that side-on view, the camera turns or rises by the least amount, one frame of its orbit at a time and no higher than an operator can hold the camera overhead, preferring views whose lens stands at its framing distance inside the ropes through the whole hold so the wide view keeps all three men, until every sightline is clear, and it holds that view through the fall and the count. A sightline is clear when, short of the struck region's own neighbourhood (the receiver's head radius), it passes outside every body that does not carry the watched point, measured on the displayed colliders.

## Measurable targets {#targets}

- Mean interval between shot-size changes between 2 and 8 s, bracketing the 2.7 s action-film average and longer broadcast holds.
- The camera is moving for at least 60% of the bout.
- The lens stays inside the ropes.
- Medium and wide shots frame both fighters' whole bodies: both fighters' feet are inside the delivered frame for at least 80% of the film, measured on the displayed ankles.
- At the finishing contact, the striking surface over its descent and the struck region are unoccluded from the delivered camera: the least sightline clearance is positive.
