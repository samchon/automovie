# Practice duelling

How two fifteen-year-olds move in a private practice wand duel, and how one hand takes another's wrist. Wand duelling is canon used as an adopted choice under the [canon boundary](../../instructions/commission.md#canon-boundary); its spells and rules are fixed in the [duelling settings](../../body/settings/world/practice-duelling.md). This study supplies the movement: the wand duel's closest real analogue is fencing, a one-handed sport in which a weapon's point is brought to a target on an opponent at speed. Source keys refer to the [research index](../index.md).

## Where the analogue holds and where it departs

Holds: a side-on stance with the weapon hand forward; footwork that keeps a chosen distance; a fast extension of the armed arm that ends with the tip aimed at the target; reactions measured in tenths of a second. Departs: a spell travels from the wand tip as a jet of light, so the target is reached at range (several metres) rather than at blade length, and there is no blade contact; a cast ends with the tip *pointing* at the target, not touching it. The lunge's footwork therefore serves balance and emphasis, not reach.

## Laws

### Stance {#stance}

Reported (chen-2017, p. 2): "for the basic 'on-guard' stance, fencers align their leading foot with their opponent's position, with the trailing foot placed at an angle to the lead foot for stability". Production's choice for the wand stance: lead foot (wand side) pointing at the opponent, rear foot about a shoulder-width and a half behind and turned out about 60–90°, knees slightly bent, trunk side-on; wand hand at chest height in front of the lead shoulder, elbow bent about 90–120°, wand tip toward the opponent's chest; the free hand relaxed at the hip or raised behind for balance.

### Cast {#cast}

A cast is a reach whose effector is the wand tip and whose target is a point on the opponent (chest for a stun, the wand hand for a disarm). Its shape follows the lunge sequence: "(A) on-guard position; (B) lifting of the lead leg; (C) forward flying phase of the lead leg and push-off with the trail leg; (D) landing of the lead foot with the armed upper limb in full extension; (E) final lunge position" (chen-2017, Fig. 2, p. 4). Skilled performers move the arm first: "Elite fencers extended the weapon arm prior to initiating front foot movement" (chen-2017, p. 17). Production's choice: a standing cast is the arm part alone — a short wrist flick (wrist extension then flexion within its range) followed by elbow extension that ends with the tip on the line to the target; a lunging cast adds a lead-foot step of 0.3–0.6 m, shorter than a fencing lunge because reach is not needed. The spell leaves the tip at the end of extension.

### Shield {#shield}

Production's choice (canon adopted): a shield is cast by the same extension with the wand held more upright and the tip sweeping a short arc in front of the body; the shield exists in front of the caster until it dissipates.

### Footwork {#footwork}

Production's choice consistent with the stance: advance lead foot first, then rear foot by the same length; retreat rear foot first, then lead foot. Steps are short (0.25–0.4 m) and keep the stance width. Reported (chen-2017, p. 17): in advance and lunge the lead foot's heel is loaded; in retreat the hallux. Retreating without looking behind is a beginner's habit and the source of falls at an edge.

### Wrist grip {#wrist-grip}

No retained source describes a coaching or rescue wrist grip ([unavailable](../index.md#unavailable)); the grip is the production's own choice built from hand anatomy (automovie-human, `packages/human/docs/anatomy/hand.md` lines 11–17 and 81). A closed hand of 170–180 mm encircles a forearm of about 150–160 mm circumference just above the wrist, thumb opposing the fingers.

- **Coaching grip.** The coach's hand arrives from outside the partner's wand arm, closes around the forearm just above the wrist crease with the thumb on the inner wrist, and turns or raises the partner's wrist to a corrected angle; the coach's other hand may rest on the partner's elbow. The hold lasts 1–3 s and opens without a pull.
- **Rescue grip.** The same grip taken on a falling person's wrist, then a pull toward the rescuer's body: the rescuer steps a foot back, drops the hips and leans away so their own weight counters the faller's. Inference: a 163 cm rescuer cannot hold the full weight of a falling person of similar size at arm's length, but can stop a fall that has just begun — while the faller still has a foot on the support or is tipping past balance — by pulling them back over their feet. The grip must therefore close in the first 0.2–0.4 s of the fall. A rescuer who starts from a standing reaction (0.3–0.6 s, the reaction times below) and must then reach (a fast reach of about 0.4–0.7 s) cannot meet that window. A rescue is possible only when the rescuer is already within arm's reach — the hand within about 0.3 m of the faller's wrist, the bodies 0.5–0.8 m apart — and already moving toward the faller before the tip begins, which happens when the rescuer foresees the fall, for example by watching a partner retreat toward an edge they cannot see.

## Outcome table {#outcome-table}

Spell effects are the adopted canon choices in the [duelling settings](../../body/settings/world/practice-duelling.md#spells); this table states which movement outcomes are possible.

| Attacker's action | Defender's state when it arrives | Outcome |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Disarming cast at the wand hand | No shield | The wand leaves the hand and flies toward the caster; the hand jerks open |
| Disarming cast | Shield up in time | The jet strikes the shield and fails; the defender keeps the wand |
| Stunning cast at the chest | No shield | In practice, the defender is knocked back one to two steps and sits or falls |
| Stunning cast | Shield up in time | The jet fails on the shield |
| Any cast | Defender steps aside before arrival | Miss; the jet passes |
| Retreat step | Rear foot finds no floor (an edge) | The retreating person tips backward and falls unless caught |

## Sequences, distance and tempo {#sequences-distance-and-tempo}

A practice duel runs in short phrases: a bow, a ready stance, two to four actions (cast, shield, step) and a reset. Reported (chen-2017, Table 2): high-intensity fencing movements average 0.7 s; lunges last under a second; elite reaction time 333 ± 128 ms against 613 ± 62 ms for lower-level fencers, total response 808 ± 53 ms against 934 ± 34 ms. Production's choice: casting range 4–5 m (the length of the duelling platform minus its ends), phrases of 3–6 s, resets of 1–3 s; coaching moments close to 0.4–0.9 m.

## Level markers {#level-markers}

Reported (chen-2017, Table 2 and p. 17): skilled fencers lunge longer (1.40 ± 0.15 m against 1.13 ± 0.13 m), move the weapon faster (peak 3.21 against 2.63 m/s) and extend the arm before the foot. Production's mapping: a skilled duellist keeps a narrow, quiet stance, casts from the wrist and elbow without a wind-up, extends before stepping, and recovers to stance at once; a less polished one casts with a wide shoulder swing, steps before extending and retreats without looking.

## Conduct {#conduct}

A private practice duel has no referee or audience; the two take turns as caster and coach, bow before beginning, and stop by lowering wands.

## Filming {#filming}

Fencing is understood along the line between the two opponents. In a 9:16 frame two people 4–5 m apart do not fit side by side at a readable size, so the line is staged in depth — one duellist near the camera, one far — or seen from above it; the wrist grip is shown in an insert or a tight two-shot that holds both forearms ([vertical short coverage](../cinematography/vertical-short-coverage.md)).
