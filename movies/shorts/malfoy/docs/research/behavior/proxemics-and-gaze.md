# Proxemics and gaze

How far apart two fifteen-year-old schoolmates stand and how long they hold each other's eyes, so that every change in distance and every look in the films reads against a measured norm. Source keys refer to the [research index](../index.md).

## Interpersonal distance {#distance}

### Law

People keep a preferred distance that shrinks as the relationship becomes closer: a stranger is kept farthest, an acquaintance nearer, a close person nearest. Stepping inside the distance one's relationship allows reads as intimacy or intrusion; stepping back out of it reads as withdrawal.

### Values

Reported measurement (sorokowska-2017, accepted manuscript p. 11, 8,943 participants in 42 countries marking on a 0–220 cm scale where another person should stop for a comfortable conversation): stranger 135.1 cm, acquaintance 91.7 cm, close person 31.9 cm, as overall means. Women kept more distance from strangers, and older people and women more from acquaintances (pp. 12–13); "younger people generally prefer closer interpersonal distances" (p. 4). The scale measures a preference marked on a diagram, not a measured standing distance.

### Use

Production's choice, built on these zones for the two leads at the shared [baseline](../../instructions/direction.md#baseline-relationship):

| Zone | Distance between the two | Reads as |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Acquaintance | 0.9–1.3 m | Their ordinary talking distance at the baseline of every film |
| Closer than acquaintances | 0.5–0.8 m | An approach that asks for something |
| Close | 0.25–0.45 m | Contact range: a hand on a wrist, two heads over one object |
| Shared bench or broom | Shoulder to shoulder, or chest to back | The films' closing closeness |

A change of zone is a beat; the screenplay decides which crossing happens and who makes it.

## Mutual gaze {#gaze}

### Law

Looking into another's eyes has a comfortable length; holding it longer becomes intense, breaking it sooner reads as shyness or avoidance. People differ in their preferred length, and the preference does not follow gender or personality in the measured sample.

### Values

Reported measurement (binetti-2016, p. 4 and Fig. 1b, 428 analysed museum visitors aged 11–79 watching clips of an actor's direct gaze lasting 0.1–10.3 s): mean preferred mutual gaze 3.3 s (SD 0.7 s), mode 2.8 s; most preferences fell between about 2 and 5 s. Participants imagined "a non-verbal interaction with a stranger as can occur on public transport" (p. 3), so the value is for strangers seen on video.

### Use

Production's choice: an ordinary look between the two lasts 2–4 s; a look Carina breaks in under 1.5 s reads as nervous; a held look over 4 s reads as charged. Where the look goes when it breaks (to the other person's hands, to the floor, away) is a character habit recorded in the cast settings.

## Conduct, level and filming

The two leads are the only people present in three of the films; in the match film the other riders keep their own play. Distance reads only when the frame contains both bodies and the floor or the space between them, and a look reads in a shot that sees both faces or cuts between matched eyelines (see [vertical short coverage](../cinematography/vertical-short-coverage.md)).
