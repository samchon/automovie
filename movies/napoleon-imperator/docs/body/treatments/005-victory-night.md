On the afternoon of December 2, the loss of the center presses on the retreat of Allied troops who had moved south. Rearguards including Dokhturov and Kienmayer cover the withdrawal of remaining forces, while narrow causeways and villages, ponds and mud restrict the routes to safety. Defeat is visible to the audience, but the army does not become a single crowd fleeing at the same moment. In the north Bagration also withdraws his troops after orders reach him.

The danger of ponds and ice remains a real source of fear. Do not turn the number of drowned claimed by a contemporary bulletin published after the battle into bodies counted by the audience that day. Preserve the result that surviving units can withdraw in an organized fashion, so the enemy's return in the next war is not a magical resurrection of a dead army.

That night the French army becomes certain of victory. For those who held against the attack, waited for support, or care for the wounded, victory brings different forms of exhaustion and absence. Napoleon has proved his military ability, but he is not yet an accountant who knows the total losses. Give the audience no verdict that cancels victory; leave alongside its name those who will not return.

Do not invent a reconciliation that seals Lannes's and Soult's feelings into friendship. Their respective achievements do not automatically settle a private insult. Stop while negotiations and treaties still lie ahead. The first section ends with the night of a victorious army; the next file returns to 1769 to discover how these names and positions came into being.
