# The late-spring 661 council announcing decisive battle

Canon guide: [Location of this subject](../outline/index.md#050-systems--035-late-spring-661-decision).

## The late-spring 661 council announcing decisive battle {#late-spring-661-decision}

**Definition and scope**

This sets the opening frame. In late spring 661 a Liaodong report reaches Pyongyang: Tang has appointed commanders for six directions and is assembling 35 expeditionary armies. Goguryeo estimates about 350,000 from that organization. After receiving it, Yeon Gaesomun announces his strategy in the [palace](../060-places/010-pyeongyang-palace.md#pyeongyang-palace) council hall. He will not repeat earlier eras’ defense upon defense while waiting for an exhausted enemy to withdraw. Fortresses, rivers, and winter will obstruct and exhaust the enemy and sever its connections while he combines dispersed forces; when the enemy is spent and divided, he will initiate decisive field battles and defeat all six forces one by one. Divide the enemy and combine our own. He gives only the broad arrangement: he will carry cavalry with him and obtain infantry from regional fortresses. The second-war phase owns detailed organization and deployment. Tang is [among the finest field armies under heaven](010-tang-military-confidence.md#tang-military-confidence); one column is comparable in size to all the field force Goguryeo can concentrate ([availability and losses](040-goguryeo-661-strength.md#goguryeo-661-strength)). That cavalry-heavy force is costly and precious, impossible to remake if lost. The decision requires defeating similarly sized elites one at a time six times, a mission so difficult it looks reckless. All six are targets from the outset. The report route and each character’s knowledge follow [campaign timing](../020-world/030-second-war-calendar.md#second-war-calendar) and [Yeon’s rule](../040-agents/001-yeon-gaesomun.md#yeon-rule).

**Established facts**

King Bojang and central military nobles and generals hear him. Bojang sits north, but the decision belongs to daemangniji Yeon Gaesomun. Eldest son [Namsaeng](../040-agents/007-namsaeng.md#namsaeng-before-succession), mangniji and commander-in-chief of three armies, stands beside his father; [Namgeon](../040-agents/008-namgeon.md#namgeon) and [Namsan](../040-agents/009-namsan.md#namsan) attend. [Go Hwi](../040-agents/032-go-hwi-flight.md#go-hwi-flight), a noble responsible for Pyongyang defense, stands in front.

Go Hwi opposes him. He survived Jupilsan in 645 and cites the record that Goguryeo won almost no field battles against Tang from 645 to 659, losing even against similar numbers and sometimes fewer troops underestimated as weak. Examples include 40,000 relieving Liaodong in 645 initially pressing Li Daozong’s 4,000 vanguard cavalry before losing badly when Li Shiji’s main force joined, and advancing against fewer Tang troops at Gwidansu in 655 and losing. Goguryeo lost at Liaodong relief, Jupilsan, Jeokriseong, relief outside Bakjakseong, Sinseong, Gwidansu, and Hoengsan; it held in fortress defenses such as Ansi, Bakjakseong, and the 658 defense. [Field records after 645](235-field-records-after-645.md#field-records-after-645) own those values. Memory of defeating Sui cannot measure Tang. One enemy column equals the entire field force Goguryeo can gather, and Goguryeo has lost to comparable numbers. More enemy columns mean more directions for a field army to travel, longer connections, and a longer period with the capital empty. His proposal to bring central field troops inside is a reasonable judgment from the same records, not fear. Even nobles who do not speak like Go Hwi see no easy field battle; nobody considers the strategy easy.

Opponents cite more than Tang’s strength and Goguryeo’s defeats. They ask whether an operation so complex and difficult can unfold as planned: who recognizes enemy exhaustion, whether distant fortresses and local forces can join in time, whether fortresses can hold until then, and whether a single delay or defeat lets other columns reach the capital. Go Hwi asks first; other nobles lean toward his questions. Gaesomun’s central answer is the light cavalry he has long developed. Led by Malgal among others, they cut communications and supply, hold enemies in place to buy time, and connect scattered friendly forces. This does not erase all opposition.

His case includes [the trap and seasonal deadline](050-forced-field-war.md#forced-field-war): field battle risks defeat, but holding only walls lets fields and harvests be stripped each year until the country dries from within. Tang’s alternating small frontier raids after 645 served that purpose. If Tang arrives in summer, holds the harvest, and stays through winter, people cannot sow in spring. Therefore hold until the enemy tires at winter’s end, then fight decisively and finish before spring. This is Gaesomun’s prediction from prior wars at the frame’s time; he does not know the actual campaign. Go Hwi does not deny the trap. His case is not “walls guarantee survival” but “field battle is a faster road to ruin”: walls dry the country slowly, while one field loss destroys it at once.

Gaesomun understands how those present receive his decision. He denies neither Tang strength nor Goguryeo’s field defeats nor the impossibility of rebuilding a lost cavalry army, but accepts them and reaches a different conclusion. Both sides use fortresses; the divide is whether to seek or avoid field decision. He aims for an unprecedented field victory. The council does not reach consensus. Some worried about the plan already know the trap firsthand: after 645 Tang repeatedly landed by sea or entered with cavalry, ravaged fields, and left; northwestern frontier and coastal farmland were ruined year after year. [Field records](235-field-records-after-645.md#field-records-after-645) own those fights. Unsure the plan will work, some almost resign themselves to a reckoning because blocking alone will not end the raids, especially nobles with people and land in those regions. Go Hwi still opposes to the end: a field loss means immediate ruin. His opposition remains unbroken. The daemangniji decides, without consensus, on a course accepted for lack of another way and with no guarantee of success.

That night Gaesomun remains alone in the office of [the Yeon house](../060-places/020-yeon-residence.md#yeon-residence). The Liaodong report lies on a low table below the south window; one lamp burns. Family quarters lie beyond a corridor, leaving little outside sound. There he addresses inwardly the king [Go Geonmu](../040-agents/015-geonmu.md#geonmu-last-defence) whom he killed. He does not speak aloud.

**Conditions and limits**

The youth phase includes this one day: arrival of the report, the council announcing the strategy and Go Hwi’s first opposition, and the night’s inward address. Later repeated opposition and rulings, his family’s evacuation and gate judgment, and actual operations belong to campaign treatments. The second-war phase closes the frame with this scene. Seats and attendees, choice of Go Hwi’s grounds, and the night office are invented; the inward words are not fixed.
